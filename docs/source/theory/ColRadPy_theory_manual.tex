\section{Purpose and model scope}\label{sec:intro}

ColRadPy converts level-resolved atomic data into populations, photon emission
coefficients, and effective rates for charge-state evolution. The basic input
is an ADAS ADF04 description of one ion: level energies, radiative probabilities,
electron-impact collision strengths, and any supplied parent-resolved ionization,
recombination, or charge-exchange data. ColRadPy implements the generalized
collisional radiative (GCR) approach~\cite{johnson,summers2006}.
Statements about the implementation refer to the source used to generate the
figures and checks of this manual~\cite{source}.

The plasma parameters are prescribed. Electrons are Maxwellian at a specified
temperature, atomic processes are represented by rate coefficients, and emitted
photons escape. Ground and metastable populations can be retained as independent
drivers. The principal calculation does not solve radiative transfer, a spatial
transport equation, or the electron distribution function. Its rate coefficients
can instead be passed to a transport or plasma model.

The collisional radiative regime lies between two useful limits. At sufficiently
low density, excitation is balanced mainly by radiative decay, giving the
coronal limit. At high density, reversible collisions tend toward a Boltzmann
distribution within a charge stage; ionization and three-body recombination
also tend toward Saha balance when their inverse processes and statistical
weights are consistent. These are limits to test, not assumptions that every
fusion or astrophysical plasma automatically satisfies.

\subsection{Two calculations with different meanings}

The quasistatic calculation sets derivatives of selected excited populations
to zero while retaining the ground and metastables as drivers. It produces
linear response coefficients. The fully time-dependent calculation propagates
the entire supplied rate matrix from an initial vector. A separate
ionization-balance calculation joins effective coefficients from adjacent
charge stages. Keeping these distinctions prevents an excited-state
response per parent from being mistaken for a normalized ion fraction.

\section{Notation, indexing and units}\label{sec:units}

Let $N_i^{(z)}$ denote the number density of level $i$ in charge state $z$.
Indices $i,j$ generally label bound levels; $\rho,\sigma$ label retained
ground or metastable levels; $p,q$ label parents in charge state $z+1$.
The subscripts $e$ and $r$ on matrix blocks denote eliminated excited and
retained states, respectively. They do not denote electron density.

The defining matrix convention is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:matrix-convention}
 \frac{\dd N_i}{\dd t}=\sum_j C_{ij}N_j,
 \qquad C_{ij}:\ j\longrightarrow i.
\end{equation}
Thus a column is a source state and a row is a destination. Off-diagonal
rates are nonnegative for a physical model. This convention differs from
some stored coefficient arrays, as listed in Section~\ref{sec:arrays}.

The conversions needed repeatedly are
\begin{align}
 \Delta E_{\rm eV}&=\frac{\Delta E_{\rm cm^{-1}}}{8065.54}, &
 T_{\rm K}&\simeq11604.5\,T_{\rm eV},\label{eq:conversion}\\
 \lambda_{\rm vac,nm}&=\frac{10^7}{|E_u-E_\ell|_{\rm cm^{-1}}}, &
 n_{\rm m^{-3}}&=10^6n_{\rm cm^{-3}}.
\end{align}
The code uses several slightly different rounded energy-conversion constants.
For reproducibility, the exact expressions in a routine take precedence over
a claim that all those constants are identical.

ADF04 level and parent identifiers are one-based. Python level indices are
zero-based; \code{metas=[0,2]} retains the first and third bound levels.
The complement is then $[1,3,4,\ldots]$, not the contiguous interval beginning
at the number of metastables. A parent identifier is also not interchangeable
with the position at which that identifier was discovered in a file.

\section{Atomic data and rate coefficients}\label{sec:rates}

\subsection{Level structure and statistical weights}

The ADF04 reader stores configurations, multiplicities, orbital labels,
energies, and a weight-related field \code{atomic['w']}. The matrix builder
uses $g_i=2w_i+1$. In an LS-resolved file the level weight is
$(2S+1)(2L+1)$; in a resolved-$J$ description it is $2J+1$.
The parent term must be interpreted consistently with that resolution.
The header provides parent ionization thresholds, while level annotations
describe parentage channels. They need not enumerate identical sets of
observed pathways~\cite{adf04}.

\begin{implnote}[Parent weights]
\routine{parent_statistical_weight} parses LS and resolved-$J$ parent terms,
including fractional $J$, and uses $g_p=(2S+1)(2L+1)$ or $g_p=2J+1$ as
appropriate. Header parent counts and discovered pathway IDs still require
care: an entry can have explicit ionization data even when its parentage
annotations are incomplete.
\end{implnote}

\subsection{Maxwellian electron-impact excitation}

For an electron-energy distribution $f_E(E)$ normalized to unity,
\begin{equation}
 q_{\ell u}=\int_{\Delta E}^{\infty}\sigma_{\ell u}(E)
       \sqrt{\frac{2E}{m_e}}\,f_E(E)\,\dd E .
\end{equation}
The tabulated effective collision strength $\Upsilon_{\ell u}(T)$ packages
the Maxwellian average in a convenient dimensionless form. The implemented
rate conversion for $u>\ell$ is
\begin{align}
 q_{u\ell}(\Te)&=\frac{2.1716\times10^{-8}}{g_u}
        \sqrt{\frac{13.6058}{\Te}}\,\Upsilon_{\ell u}(\Te),\label{eq:qdown}\\
 q_{\ell u}(\Te)&=\frac{g_u}{g_\ell}
       \exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta E}{\Te}\right)q_{u\ell}(\Te).
       \label{eq:qup}
\end{align}
Here $\Delta E$ and $\Te$ have the same energy units. Both coefficients
are in $\mathrm{cm}^3\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$. Their ratio enforces collisional
detailed balance: $N_u/N_\ell=(g_u/g_\ell)\exp(-\Delta E/\Te)$ when
collisions dominate and no other mechanism fixes the ratio.

\begin{implnote}
\routine{make_electron_excitation_rates} interpolates $\Upsilon$ onto the
user grid. \routine{populate_cr_matrix} constructs the upward and downward
rates and multiplies them by electron density. The excitation, ionization and
three-body Boltzmann factors convert cm$^{-1}$ to eV with $hc/e$
(1/8065.544), so excitation alone drives the populations to the Boltzmann
distribution at high density; the ECIP exponent keeps the constants of
\routine{r8ecip}.
\end{implnote}

\subsection{Spontaneous decay, ionization and recombination}

For a radiative transition $u\to\ell$, the population-transfer rate is
$A_{u\ell}N_u$, with no electron-density multiplier. The total radiative
loss is $\Gamma_u=\sum_{\ell<u}A_{u\ell}$.

Let $S_{ip}$ denote ionization from bound level $i$ to parent $p$. The
reduced ionization quantity in the supported ADF04 convention is converted as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reduced-ion}
 S_{ip}(\Te)=\widetilde S_{ip}(\Te)
            \exp[-\chi_{ip}/\Te],\qquad \chi_{ip}=I_p-E_i.
\end{equation}
An interpolated reduced coefficient is not itself the final ionization rate.
The input parentage factor can be applied through \code{scale_file_ioniz},
which multiplies the interpolated reduced coefficients whatever the
interpolation; the ionization balance passes the option to each stage.
Some files hold placeholders instead of rates at their lowest temperatures,
where the rate underflowed when the file was made (reduced ionization rates of
$10^{-2}$ to $10^{40}$\,cm$^3$\,s$^{-1}$ in OpenADAS neon files). Reduced
ionization rates above $10^{-6}$\,cm$^3$\,s$^{-1}$ and recombination rates above
$10^{-7}$\,cm$^3$\,s$^{-1}$, far above physical values, are left out with a
warning, and those temperatures take the rates from the other points of the
transition. Used literally, they reach three-body recombination through
detailed balance. Smaller artifacts stay below these bounds: values at the
lowest temperatures that fall towards the next ones faster than any rate of the
kind, such as a recombination coefficient of $2.6\times10^{-8}$ followed by
$10^{-30}$\,cm$^3$\,s$^{-1}$ in OpenADAS \texttt{cop98\#10\_ls\#ne1}. Recombination
falls at most as $T^{-3/2}$ (radiative recombination into levels bound by less
than $kT$, dielectronic recombination above its resonances), and reduced
ionization rises as $T^{1/2}$ near threshold and falls as about $T^{-1/2}$ far
above it. Points at the lowest temperatures that fall faster than $T^{-2}$
(recombination) or $T^{-1}$ (reduced ionization) towards a later point are
left out with a warning, from the lowest up to the first that does not.

Radiative and dielectronic recombination supplied by a file are collected in
$\alpha_{ip}^{\rm RR+DR}$. The effective recombination coefficient used at a
particular density is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:recomb}
 R_{ip}(\Te,\nelec)=\alpha_{ip}^{\rm RR+DR}(\Te)
                       +\nelec\alpha_{ip}^{(3)}(\Te).
\end{equation}
Its contribution to a matrix element is $\nelec R_{ip}$, so the three-body
term enters with two factors of electron density. Zero or missing file data
must be distinguished from a reliable physical assertion that a process is absent.

ECIP is an optional approximate completion of missing ionization channels.
\routine{ecip_rates} prepares the allowed parent channels, the levels marked
$\{p\}$ in the level list that lie below the ionization potential of parent $p$
(also above the first ionization potential), and passes Kelvin
temperatures to \routine{r8ecip}. \routine{suppliment_with_ecip} chooses
channels using parent annotations and zero entries in the assembled
ionization array. This is an approximation to missing atomic data, not a
substitute for a convergence study or a higher-quality calculated rate.

\subsection{Three-body recombination and Saha detailed balance}\label{sec:saha}

Equilibrium of $i+e\rightleftharpoons p+e+e$ requires
\begin{equation}
 N_i^*\nelec S_{ip}=N_p^*\nelec^2\alpha_{ip}^{(3)}.
\end{equation}
Using the electron translational partition factor gives
\begin{align}
 \frac{\alpha_{ip}^{(3)}}{S_{ip}}
 &=\frac{g_i}{g_p}\frac{h^3}{2(2\pi m_e k_BT_{\rm K})^{3/2}}
                   \exp\!\left(\frac{\chi_{ip}}{\Te}\right)\nonumber\\
 &\simeq \frac{g_i}{g_p}\frac{1.6564\times10^{-22}}{\Te^{3/2}}
                   \exp\!\left(\frac{\chi_{ip}}{\Te}\right)
                   \units{cm^3}.\label{eq:saha}
\end{align}
The last numerical form takes $\Te$ in eV and includes the free-electron
spin factor of two. The implementation uses the close approximation
$1.656742\times10^{-22}$.

\begin{implnote}[Stable low-temperature evaluation]
Three-body recombination uses the reduced ionization coefficient directly,
avoiding a product of an underflowed ionization rate and an overflowing
inverse exponential. This gives a finite positive result at a fixed positive
temperature when the reduced coefficient is finite and positive; it does not
establish a universal $T\to0$ limit.
\end{implnote}

\begin{implnote}[Standalone LTE helpers]
\routine{boltzmann_population} returns ground-relative level populations or
total-normalized fractions. \routine{saha_population} includes both stage
partition functions, ionization energy, electron mass, and electron density;
it returns $N_{z+1}/N_z$ or the two-stage ionized fraction. Energies are in
cm$^{-1}$, temperature in eV, and electron density in cm$^{-3}$.
\end{implnote}

\subsection{Thermal charge exchange}

A donor species at temperature $T_h$ and density $\nh$ can capture an
electron into bound level $i$ from parent $p$. Writing its coefficient
$K_{ip}(T_h)$, the gain is $\nh K_{ip}N_p^{\rm CX}$. The density multiplier
is the donor density, not automatically the electron density. The routine
\routine{make_cx_rates_from_file} interpolates the donor-temperature grid.
The user-supplied donor and electron grids must be compatible with the selected
grid or paired layout.

\section{Interpolation and asymptotic continuation}\label{sec:interpolation}

The interpolation option parser separates a leading \code{log} or
\code{loglog} flag from the SciPy interpolation kind. The \code{log} flag
selects \emph{logarithmic temperature coordinates}; it does not take the
logarithm of the rate coefficient. For example,
\begin{equation}
 \widehat r(\Te)=\mathcal I\!\left[\ln T_k,r_k\right](\ln\Te)
\end{equation}
still interpolates the ordinates $r_k$ directly. Log-temperature
interpolation alone therefore does not guarantee positive extrapolated rates:
the extrapolation is clipped at zero, which the Be\,I recombination and
ionization coefficients reached a few times above the file temperatures.

By default (\code{loglog_slinear}), recombination and reduced ionization
coefficients are interpolated as
\begin{equation}
 \widehat r(\Te)=\exp\mathcal I\!\left[\ln T_k,\ln r_k\right](\ln\Te),
\end{equation}
over the positive values of each transition. Between two temperatures this
gives the geometric mean of their values where linear interpolation of $r_k$
gives the arithmetic mean, which is never smaller and overestimates
coefficients that change by orders of magnitude between neighbouring
temperatures (by up to 45 times for OpenADAS Ne$^{7+}$ and 236 times for Be\,I
recombination). Outside the file temperatures the coefficients follow a power
law through the two nearest values, $\widehat r\propto T^{\,s}$, with the
exponent limited to physical values: $-3/2\le s\le0$ for recombination, so it
neither grows nor falls faster than radiative and dielectronic recombination;
for reduced ionization $-1\le s\le1/2$ above the file temperatures and
$s\ge0$ below them, where a falling coefficient is held constant. The options
without \code{loglog} keep the interpolation of the coefficients themselves;
excitation and charge exchange use them, and excitation is continued outside the
file temperatures as described below.

The Burgess--Tully helpers compress an unbounded temperature coordinate into
$0\le x<1$. With $\theta=k_BT/\Delta E$ and a positive scaling parameter
$c$ (default 1.5), the four implemented transforms are
\begin{table}[htbp]\centering
\begin{tabular}{cll}\toprule Type & $x(\theta)$ & Transformed ordinate $y$\\\midrule
1 & $1-\ln c/\ln(\theta+c)$ & $\Upsilon/\ln(\theta+\ee)$\\
2 & $\theta/(\theta+c)$ & $\Upsilon$\\
3 & $\theta/(\theta+c)$ & $(1+\theta)\Upsilon$\\
4 & $1-\ln c/\ln(\theta+c)$ & $\Upsilon/\ln(\theta+c)$\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Coordinate and ordinate definitions in \code{burgess_tully_rates.py}.
The original routines use Kelvin and wavenumbers to form $\theta$.}
\end{table}
An available infinite-energy point constrains the transformed high-temperature
continuation. Otherwise the code fits the final calculated points in
transformed space. The inverse transform is necessary to recover $\Upsilon$.
The end of a calculation is a limit of the dataset, not of ColRadPy: the Mo\,I
collision data end near 17\,eV and are continued above it with the
Burgess--Tully transforms~\cite{johnson}. The
infinite-energy information constrains a continuation but does not validate
an extrapolation far from the calculated range.

\begin{implnote}[Continuation]
The caller applies all four high-temperature categories and uses one aligned
fallback slot per category. Below the file grid it applies a nonnegative
transformed continuation or retains the first collision strength for
transitions whose data start far above threshold. This numerical policy
prevents negative rates but does not establish atomic-data accuracy outside
the calculated range.
\end{implnote}

\section{Atomic data from the Flexible Atomic Code}\label{sec:fac}

The Flexible Atomic Code (FAC)~\cite{fac} computes level energies, radiative
rates and cross-sections of electron collisions in a $jj$-coupled basis.
\routine{read_fac} reads the ASCII tables that its \code{PrintTable} writes
with energies in eV into the ADF04 dictionary of Section~\ref{sec:rates}, so
the matrix of Section~\ref{sec:matrix} is built as for an ADF04 file and the
solver is unchanged. The levels of the ion are sorted by energy; those of the
next ion are the parents, with $I_p$ measured from the ion's ground. FAC levels
have an exact $J$ but no LS term: their $L$ and $S$ are $-1$, and the parent
weights of Eq.~\eqref{eq:saha} are $2J_p+1$.

\subsection{Maxwellian averages from each threshold}

A process with threshold $E_0$ has the rate coefficient
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fac-rate}
 q(T)=\int_{E_0}^{\infty}\sigma(E)\,v(E)\,f(E,T)\,\dd E
     =e^{-E_0/T}\,T\int_0^\infty\sigma(E)\,v(E)\,\tilde f(E,T)\,e^{-u}\,\dd u,
 \qquad E=E_0+uT,
\end{equation}
with $\tilde f=f\,e^{E/T}$. The distribution is Maxwell--J\"uttner,
\begin{equation}
 f(E,T)=\frac{\gamma^2\beta\,e^{-\gamma/\theta}}{\theta K_2(1/\theta)\,mc^2},
 \qquad \gamma=1+\frac{E}{mc^2},\quad \theta=\frac{T}{mc^2},\quad v=c\beta,
\end{equation}
which is $2\sqrt{E/\pi}\,T^{-3/2}e^{-E/T}$ for $T\ll mc^2$. The last integral
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:fac-rate} is taken with 64 Gauss--Laguerre nodes, so the
nodes start at the threshold and follow the Boltzmann factor whatever $E_0/T$,
and $\tilde f$ uses the exponentially scaled Bessel function, so nothing
underflows. The reduced coefficient $q\,e^{E_0/T}$ gives the effective
collision strength $\Upsilon=q\,e^{\Delta E/T}g_\ell\sqrt{T/13.6058}/
2.1716\times10^{-8}$, with the constants of Eq.~\eqref{eq:qdown} so that the
matrix holds the integrated rate, and the reduced ionization rate of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:reduced-ion}. The cross-sections follow from FAC's tables as FAC
defines them, with $k^2=2E(1+\alpha^2E/2)$ and $E$ in hartree:
$\sigma=\pi a_0^2\Omega/(g_\ell k^2)$ for excitation, $a_0^2\Omega/(g k^2)$ for
ionization, and, from the bound-free oscillator strength density $S$,
$\sigma_{\rm RR}=2\pi\alpha S a_0^2(\alpha E_\gamma)^2/
[2\varepsilon(1+\alpha^2\varepsilon/2)g_p]$ for radiative recombination of a
free electron of energy $\varepsilon$ with a photon $E_\gamma$.

Between the tabulated energies $\Omega$ and $S$ are interpolated log-log.
Below the first one $\Omega$ of excitation and $S$ are held, as they are finite
at threshold, and $\Omega$ of ionization follows FAC's fit. Above the last,
excitation joins FAC's Bethe--Born limit $\Omega=b\ln(E/E_0)+c$ at the energy
FAC computed it for (FAC's manual advises against its direct values at hundreds
of thresholds), and a transition without a Born limit (exchange) follows the
power law of its last two values, at least as steep as $E^{-1}$, as FAC does;
ionization follows FAC's fit
$\Omega=p_0\ln x+p_1y^2+p_2y/x+p_3y/x^2$ ($x=E/I$, $y=1-1/x$), and
recombination FAC's fit of $S$, valid at high energies, with the angular
momentum of the captured electron, both scaled to the last tabulated value.

\subsection{Autoionization and dielectronic recombination}

Dielectronic capture into an autoionizing level $d$ from parent $p$ has the
strength $S_{\rm DC}$ (cm$^2$\,eV) at the resonance energy $E_r=E_d-I_p$, so
$\alpha_{\rm DC}=S_{\rm DC}\,v(E_r)\,f(E_r,T)$, which for a Maxwellian is the
detailed balance of the autoionization rate $A^a_{dp}$,
$\alpha_{\rm DC}=\frac{g_d}{2g_p}\bigl(h^2/2\pi mT\bigr)^{3/2}A^a_{dp}e^{-E_r/T}$.
ADF04 data have no autoionization channel, so \routine{_fold_autoionization}
folds it into the other rates. With $R_{d\ell}$ the radiative rates of $d$,
$R_d=\sum_\ell R_{d\ell}$ and $A^a_d=\sum_pA^a_{dp}$, from the lowest
autoionizing level up,
\begin{equation}
 b_d=\frac{\sum_\ell R_{d\ell}\,b_\ell}{R_d+A^a_d},\qquad
 P_{dp}=\frac{A^a_{dp}+\sum_\ell R_{d\ell}P_{\ell p}}{R_d+A^a_d},
\end{equation}
with $b=1$ and $P=0$ for levels that do not autoionize: $b_d$ is the fraction
of the population of $d$ that ends in stable levels and $P_{dp}$ the fraction
that reaches parent $p$. Every flow into $d$ (capture, excitation and cascades,
$R_{d'd}\to b_dR_{d'd}$) is multiplied by $b_d$, and the excitation of a stable
level $\ell$ into $d$ that autoionizes becomes its ionization
(excitation-autoionization),
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde S_{\ell p}\mathrel{+}=\tilde q_{\ell d}\,P_{dp}\,
 e^{-(E_d-I_p)/T},
\end{equation}
with $\tilde q_{\ell d}$ the reduced excitation rate. The population of $d$
is then the inflow over $R_d+A^a_d$, as with the autoionization in the matrix,
while collisions from $d$ are slow beside its decays (below about
$10^{20}$\,cm$^{-3}$ for highly charged ions), and so are its lines, the
dielectronic recombination and the ionization. Left out are the capture that
autoionizes to another parent, a process of the next ion; the emission of
lines between two autoionizing levels beyond $b$ of the lower; and
autoionizing levels without radiative decays, which never stabilize. The
two-photon decays of H-like $2s$ and He-like $1s2s\,{}^1S_0$~\cite{drake}
add to their A-values.

\begin{implnote}[Checks]
The tests write FAC tables for a toy ion and check: a constant $\Omega$ gives
$\Upsilon=\Omega$; $\Omega=b\ln x+c$ gives $c+b\,e^yE_1(y)$ with $y=E_0/T$;
a constant $S$ gives the closed-form radiative recombination; capture follows
the detailed balance above; and the folded rates give the populations, SCD and
ACD of the model with the autoionization in its matrix to $10^{-14}$. On FAC's
own reference tables the cross-sections made from $\Omega$ and $S$ are those
FAC prints to $8\times10^{-5}$. The average of Mewe's Gaunt-factor
cross-section~\cite{mewe} for Ar$^{17+}$ $1s$--$2p_{1/2}$ matches its closed form to
$5\times10^{-5}$ from 10\,eV to 85\,keV, and the relativistic average is lower
by 2\% at 10\,keV and 17\% at 85\,keV.
\end{implnote}

The averages assume a thermal distribution: ColRadPy derives de-excitation and
three-body recombination by detailed balance, which holds for no other.
Magnetic-sublevel tables are not read, and FAC parents have no LS term to
link the stages of an ionization balance.

\section{The collisional radiative matrix}\label{sec:matrix}

For bound levels $i\ne j$, define $q_{j\to i}$ as the corresponding
electron-impact coefficient. Then
\begin{align}
 C_{ij}&=A_{j\to i}+\nelec q_{j\to i},\label{eq:bound-offdiag}\\
 C_{ii}&=-\sum_{j\ne i}(A_{i\to j}+\nelec q_{i\to j})
                  -\nelec\sum_pS_{ip}.\label{eq:bound-diag}
\end{align}
Here forbidden or upward spontaneous transitions have $A=0$.
For adjacent-charge parents,
\begin{equation}
 C_{p i}=\nelec S_{ip},\qquad C_{i p}=\nelec R_{ip}.
\end{equation}
The corresponding parent diagonal loses recombination into the lower stage.
A closed collection of states satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:conservation}
 \sum_i C_{ij}=0\quad\hbox{for every source column }j,
 \qquad \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\sum_i N_i=0.
\end{equation}
Column sums need not vanish in a deliberately open subsystem; the missing
population must then be accounted for as an external source or sink.

\begin{implnote}
\routine{populate_cr_matrix} stores the main generator in
\code{cr_matrix['cr']}; \code{A_ji} is stored with upper-level index first,
so radiative entries are transposed when inserted into the generator.
\code{cr_red} later contains only the selected excited-state block.
The separate \code{cr_loss} array holds only the level-loss diagonal of
that generator.
\end{implnote}

The CX driving columns used by the quasistatic solution have no matching
losses: they attribute emission to CX from a given parent population.
\routine{solve_time_dependent} instead moves the CX gains to the
recombining-parent columns and subtracts them from the parent diagonal, so
CX depletes the evolving parent and the transient generator satisfies
Eq.~\eqref{eq:conservation}. The CX columns are then empty; their initial
populations and sources must be zero.

The off-diagonal sign, column sums, and inverse-channel density powers are
useful checks before any matrix is solved. A linear algebra routine can solve
a matrix with negative or incorrectly scaled atomic rates without identifying
the underlying physical error.

\section{Projection of the high \texorpdfstring{$n$}{n}-shells}\label{sec:projection}

The levels of an ADF04 file usually cover the lowest principal shells,
$n\le n_1$. The levels of higher shells, up to the series limits of the
parents, are missing from the matrix of Section~\ref{sec:matrix}, yet most
recombination passes through them: radiative capture into high $n$,
dielectronic capture into Rydberg states, and the cascades that follow. At
high density they also open stepwise ionization. For neon, the effective
recombination into Ne$^{3+}$, Ne$^{4+}$ and Ne$^{5+}$ from the ADAS
\texttt{cop98\#10} ADF04 files alone is 0.17--0.69 of the ADAS coefficients made
from the same files with the high shells included (5--100\,eV), and the SCD of
Ne$^{2+}$ at 1\,eV and $10^{14}$\,cm$^{-3}$ is 0.007 of the ADAS one.

\subsection{Condensed bundle-\texorpdfstring{$nS$}{nS} couplings}

A bundle-$nS$ calculation (ADAS204) groups the levels built on a parent
$\rho$ of the next ion with spin system $S$ into $n$-shell populations
$x_n$, from the lowest shell to the series limit, and eliminates all but its
lowest shells. The condensed couplings are given for each parent and spin
system on a temperature--density grid (ADF17 files), in the convention of
losses positive:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bundle}
 \sum_m P_{nm}\,x_m=\nelec N_\rho\, r_n ,
\end{equation}
where $P_{nm}$ (s$^{-1}$) is the coupling from shell $m$ to shell $n$
($P_{nn}$ the total loss of shell $n$), $r_n$ (cm$^3$\,s$^{-1}$) the
recombination into shell $n$ by all routes, $I_{n\rho'}$ (s$^{-1}$) the
ionization of shell $n$ to parent $\rho'$, and $Q_{\rho'}$
(cm$^3$\,s$^{-1}$) the recombination from $\rho$ that ionizes to another
parent $\rho'$ without reaching the lowest shells. The same files give the
direct parts: the shell couplings $D_{nm}$, direct ionization $D^{\rm I}_{n\rho'}$
and direct radiative, dielectronic, three-body and charge-exchange
recombination $d_n$.

Shells $u$ above the highest shell of the ADF04 levels are eliminated from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:bundle} onto the kept shells $l$ (a Schur complement):
\begin{align}
 P'&=P_{ll}-P_{lu}P_{uu}^{-1}P_{ul}, &
 r'&=r_l-P_{lu}P_{uu}^{-1}r_u,\\
 I'&=I_l-\bigl(P_{uu}^{-1}P_{ul}\bigr)^{\mathsf T} I_u, &
 Q'&=Q+I_u^{\mathsf T}P_{uu}^{-1}r_u ,
\end{align}
and the direct transfers from kept to eliminated shells become indirect. The
ADF04 file already has the direct couplings between its levels, so they are
subtracted. The indirect couplings through the high shells are then
\begin{equation}
 K=P'-D'-\mathrm{diag}\Bigl(\sum_{\rho'}D^{\rm I}\Bigr),\quad
 I^{\rm ind}=I'-D^{\rm I},\quad
 r^{\rm ind}=r'-d,\quad Q^{\rm ind}=Q'.
\end{equation}
Negative values up to $10^{-3}$ of the projected and direct values they are
the difference of are round-off (the ADF17 files have 11 digits) and are set
to zero. Larger ones show condensed data that are not reliable, as at the
lowest temperatures of most ADF17 files; those temperatures are left out of
that coupling of the parent and spin system.

\subsection{Expansion over the levels}

An ADF18 expansion file (\code{a17_p208} kind) assigns each ADF04 level $i$
to a parent, spin system and shell $n(i)$, with a weight $w_i$ normalised
over the levels of that shell (statistical weights). Level populations add
up to shell populations, so a rate per unit shell population applies to each
of its levels, while a flow into a shell is shared by its levels with their
weights:
\begin{align}
 R^{\rm ind}_{i\rho}&=w_i\,r^{\rm ind}_{n(i)}, &
 S^{\rm ind}_{i\rho}&=I^{\rm ind}_{n(i)\rho}/\nelec,\\
 G_{ij}&=-w_i\,K_{n(i)n(j)}\quad(n(i)\ne n(j)), &
 X^{\rm ind}_{\rho'\rho}&=\sum_S Q^{\rm ind}_{\rho'} .
\end{align}
Transfers within a shell are not described by shell populations and are
omitted. These terms are added to the matrix of Section~\ref{sec:matrix}:
$G_{ij}$ to $C_{ij}$, $\nelec S^{\rm ind}_{i\rho}$ to $C_{\rho i}$ and
$\nelec R^{\rm ind}_{i\rho}$ to $C_{i\rho}$, each with its loss on the
diagonal of the source, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:conservation} still holds. The
indirect parent coupling $X^{\rm ind}$ is added to XCD
(Section~\ref{sec:xcd}). Populations, PECs, SCD, ACD, QCD and XCD then
include the high shells.

Below the first temperature $T_0$ of a coupling $c$ on the ADF17 grid (without
the temperatures left out), it continues with the Boltzmann factor of its
threshold $\Delta E$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:projection-below}
 c(T)=c(T_0)\Bigl(\frac{T}{T_0}\Bigr)^{a}
      \exp\Bigl[-\Delta E\Bigl(\frac{1}{kT}-\frac{1}{kT_0}\Bigr)\Bigr],
\end{equation}
with $a$ the slope of $\ln\bigl(c\,e^{\Delta E/kT}\bigr)$ against $\ln T$
between its two lowest temperatures, limited as for the ADF04 rates
(Section~\ref{sec:interpolation}): between $-1.5$ and 0 for recombination and
the parent coupling, at least 0 for the reduced ionization and transfers.
Every indirect path out of a level starts with its excitation into the high
shells, so the threshold of $S^{\rm ind}_{i\rho}$ is the ionization energy
$\chi_i$ of level $i$; that of $G_{ij}$ is $\chi_j-\bar\chi_{n_1}n_1^2/(n_1+1)^2$,
the energy to reach the shell above the highest kept one, $n_1$, whose levels
have the mean ionization energy $\bar\chi_{n_1}$; that of $X^{\rm ind}_{\rho'\rho}$
is the energy between the parents when $\rho'$ lies above $\rho$; and
recombination has none. Above the grid the values at its edge are kept.

\begin{implnote}[Choices of the implementation]
Ionization through the shells of one parent to another parent is the
autoionization of core-excited Rydberg states, part of
excitation-autoionization, which the ionization rates of the ADF04 files
(ADF07 data) already include. It is left out. ADAS208 also credits only the
group's own parent but keeps that loss on the diagonal; ColRadPy omits both,
so the matrix conserves particles. The diagonal follows from the expanded
gains and ionization rather than from $K_{nn}$; the two agree when the
condensed data conserve particles. The condensed data are interpolated
linearly in $\ln\Te$, $\ln\nelec$ and the logarithm of each coupling, without
the temperatures where it is not reliable, and continued below the ADF17 grid
by Eq.~\eqref{eq:projection-below}, whose threshold for the transfers scales
the binding energy of the highest kept shell hydrogenically; both give a
warning when the user temperatures need them. The fractional-parentage
corrections of the lowest shell (switches \code{IEFPRE}, \code{IEFPRS}, set
only by the superseded \code{cbnm93} files) are not implemented, and
indirect charge-exchange recombination is not represented.
\end{implnote}

\begin{implnote}[Routines and data]
\routine{read_adf17} reads the condensed matrices and \routine{read_adf18}
the expansion files; \routine{projection.project} forms and expands the
indirect couplings. \code{colradpy(..., projection=(adf17, adf18))} stores
them in \code{rates['projection']} (\routine{make_projection}, called by
\routine{solve_cr} for each solve on its temperatures and densities, with the
files read once) and \routine{populate_cr_matrix} adds them to the
generator. In an ionization balance, \code{projection} takes one pair, or
\code{None}, per ADF04 file. The ADF18 file names the ADF04 and ADF17 files
it maps; its level numbers follow the order of the ADF04 file, before any
sorting by energy. Levels it maps to spin systems or shells that the ADF17
file does not have get no projection, with a warning. The recombination
rates of the ADF04 file are zero-density data; the density dependence of
dielectronic recombination through the high shells (the collisional
redistribution of doubly excited states, Eq.~45 of Summers et
al.~\cite{summers2006}) reaches ColRadPy only through the ADF17 data.
\end{implnote}

\section{Quasistatic elimination of excited levels}\label{sec:quasistatic}

Partition the population vector into retained states $N_r$ and excited states
$N_e$. The full system is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:blocks}
 \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\begin{pmatrix}N_r\\N_e\end{pmatrix}
 =\begin{pmatrix}C_{rr}&C_{re}\\C_{er}&C_{ee}\end{pmatrix}
  \begin{pmatrix}N_r\\N_e\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
When excited-state relaxation is fast compared with evolution of the drivers,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qs}
 0=C_{ee}N_e+C_{er}N_r,
 \qquad N_e=-C_{ee}^{-1}C_{er}N_r.
\end{equation}
This requires an invertible, relaxing excited block. Including a long-lived
state in the eliminated set can invalidate the approximation even if the
numerical inverse exists. Time-scale separation should be assessed from the
excited-block decay rates and the time scale of the retained dynamics.

Substitution into the retained equations gives the Schur complement
\begin{equation}\label{eq:schur}
 C_{\rm eff}=C_{rr}-C_{re}C_{ee}^{-1}C_{er}.
\end{equation}
This formula is the common origin of the effective coefficients below. For a
closed model, correctly eliminating excited states preserves column-sum
conservation in the retained equations.

\subsection{Choosing retained levels and testing time-scale separation}\label{sec:metastables}

Summers et al.~\cite{summers2006} order the intrinsic lifetimes of an ion of
charge $z$ as $\tau_a\ll\tau_o\ll\tau_m$: auto-ionizing levels
($\tau_a\sim10^{-13}$\,s), ordinary excited levels
($\tau_o\sim10^{-8}/z^4$\,s) and metastables ($\tau_m\sim10/z^8$\,s, radiative
decay). For most fusion and astrophysical plasmas
$\tau_{\rm plasma}\sim\tau_g\sim\tau_m\gg\tau_o\gg\tau_{e\mbox{-}e}$, with
$\tau_g$ the relaxation time of the ground populations by ionization and
recombination and $\tau_{e\mbox{-}e}$ the thermalization time of the free
electrons. This ordering underlies the quasistatic picture: the ground and
metastable populations evolve with the plasma and are retained, while the
excited populations and the free electrons (a Maxwellian distribution) relax
to them. Redistribution of the excited populations by collisions, on a time
$\tau_{\rm red}$, defines critical densities, $\tau_{\rm red}\sim\tau_o$ and
$\tau_{\rm red}\sim\tau_a$, which separate low levels, high singly excited
levels and doubly excited levels; the projection of
Section~\ref{sec:projection} treats the high levels.

A spectroscopically metastable level and a level that must be retained
dynamically are not the same thing~\cite{johnson}.
A weak spontaneous decay can produce a large population, but collisions and
ionization can shorten its actual relaxation time. Conversely, a collection
of ordinary excited levels can participate in a slow, strongly coupled mode.
The retained set is therefore a modeling choice for the density, temperature,
initial condition and diagnostic being considered.

For constant matrix blocks, write the instantaneous quasistatic response as
$N_e^{\rm qs}(t)=-C_{ee}^{-1}C_{er}N_r(t)$ and let
$\delta=N_e-N_e^{\rm qs}$. The exact lag equation is
\begin{align}
 \dot\delta&=C_{ee}\delta-\dot N_e^{\rm qs},\\
 \delta(t)&=\ee^{C_{ee}t}\delta(0)
       -\int_0^t\ee^{C_{ee}(t-u)}\dot N_e^{\rm qs}(u)\,\dd u.
       \label{eq:qs-lag}
\end{align}
There are two distinct errors: an initial excited-state relaxation transient
and the lag behind a changing driver. After the first decays, a small ratio
of excited relaxation time to driver-evolution time supports the
approximation. The decaying eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $C_{ee}$ help
assess this, although a nonnormal matrix can amplify transients and a gap in
eigenvalues alone is not a complete error bound. Changing plasma conditions
also changes the response matrix itself and contributes to
$\dot N_e^{\rm qs}$.

A useful practical test is to start full and reduced models from consistent
retained populations, vary the retained set, and compare the predicted line
emission over the required observation times. A ground-only quasistatic
model eliminates every other bound state, including levels that might need
their own evolving populations. A large excited population identifies a
candidate for examination, but a small population can still be an important
feeder of a diagnostic line. Eigenmodes are combinations of levels; each
eigenvalue should not be labeled as the lifetime of one level without
examining its eigenvector.

Comparing the full time-dependent model, which evolves every level, with
reductions that retain selected ground and metastable populations and
eliminate the other levels quasistatically shows that the adequacy of a
retained set depends on the species and conditions
(Table~\ref{tab:manifolds})~\cite{johnson}:

\begin{table}[htbp]\centering\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{l l X}
\toprule Published case & Plasma conditions & Reported observation\\\midrule
Be I & $\Te=20$ eV, $\nelec=10^{13}$ cm$^{-3}$ &
Retaining the important metastable substantially improves the time-dependent
line-emission prediction over a ground-only reduction.\\
Mo I & $\Te=8$ eV, $\nelec=5\times10^{11}$ cm$^{-3}$ &
Even a ten-metastable reduction does not reproduce the full
time-dependent line history.\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Retained sets compared with the full time-dependent
model~\cite{johnson}, not rerun for this manual. They do not set a universal
number of retained levels for either species.}\label{tab:manifolds}
\end{table}

The Be example exhibits a separation between slowly relaxing modes and the
faster excited manifold. The Mo example has a more gradual spectrum of
relaxation rates, making a small retained set less successful, which is what
the lag and emission-convergence tests above detect. A ground-only reduction
also loses the ability to represent an independently evolving metastable
fraction.

\subsection{Separate excitation, recombination and CX drivers}\label{sec:cxdrivers}

The excited-state forcing can be separated as
\begin{equation}
 0=C_{ee}N_e+C_{e m}N_m+\nelec R_{e p}N_p
                                +\nh K_{e p}N_p^{\rm CX}.
\end{equation}
Define the dimensionless response matrices
\begin{align}
 F&=-C_{ee}^{-1}C_{em}, &
 G&=-C_{ee}^{-1}\nelec R_{ep}, &
 H&=-C_{ee}^{-1}\nh K_{ep},\label{eq:responses}\\
 N_e&=FN_m+GN_p+HN_p^{\rm CX}.\nonumber
\end{align}
Each column states a response to one independently prescribed driving
population. These are not probabilities whose sums must all be one.

\begin{implnote}
\routine{solve_quasi_static} constructs
\code{beta}$=-C_{er}$ and computes \code{pops}$=C_{ee}^{-1}\code{beta}$.
The excitation columns come first, followed by recombination and then CX
columns when enabled. \code{processed['F']} denotes an excitation response
without the additional $1/\nelec$ used in some ADAS definitions. Matching
symbols without matching their density convention produces unit errors.
\end{implnote}

\begin{implnote}[CX-only construction]
When CX is enabled but both radiative and three-body recombination are
disabled, the CX columns still follow the parent columns, which the
generator keeps whether or not recombination is used.
\end{implnote}

\subsection{Normalization is a choice of driving population}\label{sec:normalization}

For a single ground driver, an excitation response $F_i=N_i/N_0$ can be
converted to a fraction relative to the ion total:
\begin{equation}
 \frac{N_0}{N_{\rm ion}}=\frac{1}{1+\sum_iF_i},\qquad
 \frac{N_i}{N_{\rm ion}}=\frac{F_i}{1+\sum_jF_j}.
\end{equation}
This transformation changes the denominator of an excitation coefficient.
It is not applicable without modification to $G_i=N_i/N_p$, whose parent
belongs to another charge state. The independent drivers are explicitly
distinguished in GCR emissivity definitions~\cite{adasreport}.

\begin{implnote}[Default normalization]
With one retained metastable, excitation columns are normalized by default;
recombination and CX columns remain relative to their independent parents.
With more than one retained metastable, excitation normalization defaults
off. The \code{default_pop_norm} option controls this choice. For example,
an excited level with $A=1\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ and parent-driven injection
$\nelec\alpha=2\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ retains the required response $N_e/N_p=2$.
\end{implnote}

\subsection{Unsummed contributions and dominant feeding mechanisms}\label{sec:feeding}

The individual terms of the excited-state solution identify the important
populating channels~\cite{johnson}. Let $a$ label an
excitation, recombination or CX driver, and write
$\beta_{ja}=-C_{ja}$ for the negative of its driving-block entry into
eliminated level $j$. Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:unsummed}
 U_{ija}=(C_{ee}^{-1})_{ij}\beta_{ja}
 \quad\hbox{(no sum over $j$)},\qquad
 N_i=\sum_a\sum_j U_{ija}N_a.
\end{equation}
The sum over $j$ gives the response of level $i$ to driver $a$; leaving it
unsummed assigns that response to the level where the driver injects
population into the eliminated manifold. For a fixed driver with a
nonzero total response, the fractions
\begin{equation}
 f_{j\mid i,a}=\frac{U_{ija}}{\sum_k U_{ika}}
\end{equation}
identify the injection channels carrying the largest contribution. This
interpretation requires physical nonnegative rates and a relaxing excited
block. A common normalization applied to one driver's response cancels in
these fractions, but different drivers must be weighted by their actual
populations before they are combined.

\begin{implnote}[The stored contribution tensor]
The implementation stores $U$ in \code{processed['pop_lvl']} with axes
\emph{destination excited level, injection excited level, driver,
temperature, density}. Both excited-level indices refer to the reduced
level map, not to physical bound-level numbers. Summing the second axis
reconstructs \code{processed['pops']} under the same normalization.
Paired mode replaces the last two axes by one pair index.
\end{implnote}

An entry with $j=i$ represents direct injection into the observed upper
level, followed by its full response through $C_{ee}^{-1}$. An entry with
$j\ne i$ represents injection elsewhere followed by redistribution or a
cascade. These are algebraic injection-channel contributions, not exclusive
microscopic paths or counts of the last collision: the inverse already
contains repeated transfers. For the Mo\,I lines near 379.8 and 550.6\,nm,
these terms, with population-weighted metastable contributions, separate strong
direct ground injection from feeding through other populated
levels~\cite{johnson}. An unweighted PEC column alone gives
neither line's contribution to the measured intensity, which needs the
weighting of Eq.~\eqref{eq:emissivity}.

The same accounting can resolve effective rates. For example, level $i$
contributes $S_{ip}F_{i\rho}$ to indirect ionization, and contributes
$C_{\rho i}G_{ip}/\nelec$ to capture feeding retained level $\rho$. Keeping
these terms unsummed can guide a reduced atomic model, provided its emission
and effective-rate errors are checked afterward. Bundling levels is another
way to reduce the dynamical size of heavy-species models~\cite{johnson};
choosing a short list of retained metastables is not by itself a
population-preserving bundling, and ColRadPy provides no automatic bundled
model.

\section{Generalized collisional radiative coefficients}\label{sec:gcr}

Effective coefficients combine direct processes with paths through eliminated
excited states. Their definition is a reduction of the generator, not simply
a sum of raw atomic rates. All formulas in this section assume the
unnormalized responses of Eq.~\eqref{eq:responses} and explicitly prescribed
retained populations. The stored arrays and their index order are listed in
Section~\ref{sec:arrays}.

\subsection{SCD: effective ionization}

For a retained bound level $\rho$ and destination parent $p$,
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal S_{\rho p}=S_{\rho p}+\sum_iS_{ip}F_{i\rho}.
\end{equation}
The second term describes excitation or redistribution followed by ionization.
Both terms have coefficient units. Its matrix contribution from $\rho$ to
$p$ is $\nelec\mathcal S_{\rho p}$.

\subsection{ACD and CCD: effective capture}

The capture coefficient from parent $p$ into retained level $\rho$ is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal A_{\rho p}=R_{\rho p}
              -\sum_{ij}C_{\rho i}(C_{ee}^{-1})_{ij}R_{jp}.
\end{equation}
The second term incorporates capture into excited levels followed by their
collisional redistribution and radiative cascades. Replacing $R$ by $K$
defines $\mathcal C_{\rho p}$, the CCD coefficient. The rate multipliers are
$\nelec$ and $\nh$, respectively. In an ionization matrix expressed per
electron density, the CCD entry carries $\nh/\nelec$.

\subsection{QCD: redistribution within a retained bound manifold}

For two different retained bound levels,
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal Q_{\sigma\to\rho}
 =\frac{C_{\rho\sigma}+\sum_iC_{\rho i}F_{i\sigma}}{\nelec},
 \qquad \rho\ne\sigma.
\end{equation}
This includes direct mixing and indirect paths through the excited manifold.
The diagonal is kept zero in the stored QCD array; its population loss is
inserted separately when the charge-state matrix is assembled.

\subsection{XCD: redistribution between parent metastables}\label{sec:xcd}

A parent $p$ can recombine into an excited level of the lower stage and then
ionize into a different parent $q$. The coefficient for this path is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:xcd}
 \mathcal X_{p\to q}=-\sum_{ij}C_{qi}(C_{ee}^{-1})_{ij}R_{jp}
       =-\nelec\sum_{ij}S_{iq}(C_{ee}^{-1})_{ij}R_{jp},\qquad p\ne q.
\end{equation}
The factor $\nelec$ in the second form is essential. Dimensional analysis gives
\begin{equation}
 [\nelec S][C_{ee}^{-1}][R]
  =(\mathrm{s}^{-1})(\mathrm s)(\mathrm{cm}^3\,\mathrm{s}^{-1})
  =\mathrm{cm}^3\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}.
\end{equation}
This is equivalent to dividing the parent-to-parent off-diagonal block of
the Schur complement by electron density. The recombination rows must use
the same excited-level ordering as $C_{ee}$.
The same factor is in the definition of XCD of Summers et al.\ (their
Eq.~17)~\cite{summers2006}.

\begin{implnote}[XCD contraction]
The contraction uses $\nelec S$ and selects recombination rows by the actual
eliminated-level map, including noncontiguous metastable selections such as
$[0,2]$. The resulting coefficient has units cm$^3$/s and agrees with the
parent block of the direct Schur complement divided by electron density.
\end{implnote}

\section{Derived emission and power coefficients}\label{sec:emission}

Spectrum-line and radiated-power coefficients follow from the quasistatic
responses of Eq.~\eqref{eq:responses}, as in Section~1.2.2 of Summers
et al.~\cite{summers2006}. Their responses
$\mathcal F^{\rm (exc)}_{u\rho}=F_{u\rho}/\nelec$ and
$\mathcal F^{\rm (rec)}_{up}=G_{up}/\nelec$ include the electron density that
the $F$ and $G$ of ColRadPy leave out (Section~\ref{sec:cxdrivers}).

\subsection{Photon emissivity coefficients}

For an optically thin line $u\to\ell$, the angle-integrated photon
emissivity divides by driving population, a metastable $\rho$ of the ion or a
metastable $p$ of the next ion (the parent):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:emissivity}
 \epsilon_{u\ell}=A_{u\ell}N_u
 =\nelec\sum_\rho N_\rho\mathrm{PEC}^{\rm exc}_{u\ell,\rho}
  +\nelec\sum_pN_p\mathrm{PEC}^{\rm rec}_{u\ell,p}
  +\nh\sum_pN_p^{\rm CX}\mathrm{PEC}^{\rm CX}_{u\ell,p}
 \quad[\mathrm{photons}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-3}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}].
\end{equation}
This defines the excitation, recombination and charge-exchange photon
emissivity coefficients (cm$^3$\,s$^{-1}$),
\begin{align}
 \mathrm{PEC}^{\rm exc}_{u\ell,\rho}&=A_{u\ell}F_{u\rho}/\nelec,\label{eq:pec-exc}\\
 \mathrm{PEC}^{\rm rec}_{u\ell,p}&=A_{u\ell}G_{up}/\nelec,\label{eq:pec-rec}\\
 \mathrm{PEC}^{\rm CX}_{u\ell,p}&=A_{u\ell}H_{up}/\nh.\label{eq:pec-cx}
\end{align}
The upper level can also be a retained metastable $\sigma$. Its population
relative to the driver $\rho$ is $\delta_{\sigma\rho}$, so the lines it emits
to the levels below it, the forbidden transitions between metastables of
Summers et al.\ (their Eq.~26), have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pec-meta}
 \mathrm{PEC}^{\rm exc}_{\sigma\ell,\rho}=\delta_{\sigma\rho}A_{\sigma\ell}/\nelec,
 \qquad
 \mathrm{PEC}^{\rm rec}_{\sigma\ell,p}=\mathrm{PEC}^{\rm CX}_{\sigma\ell,p}=0.
\end{equation}
With the populations normalized to the whole ion
(Section~\ref{sec:normalization}) an excitation PEC is multiplied by
$N_\rho/N_{\rm ion}$. This is the coefficient for a stage-to-stage
ionization balance, where a metastable treated as an ordinary level can have
a quasistatic population comparable to that of the ground~\cite{summers2006}.
For an isotropic emitter, a line-of-sight photon radiance is
$\int\epsilon_{u\ell}\,\dd s/(4\pi)$. The $4\pi$ factor is an observation
convention and is not already part of $A_{u\ell}N_u$. The ionizations per
emitted photon, S/XB, are defined from the excitation PECs in
Section~\ref{sec:sxb}.

\begin{implnote}[CX PEC convention]
The implementation divides \emph{all} computed driving columns by $\nelec$ when forming
\code{processed['pecs']}. Its stored CX columns therefore contain
$A H/\nelec=(\nh/\nelec)\mathrm{PEC}^{\rm CX}$ as defined above.
Use electron density with that stored response, or explicitly convert to a
donor-normalized coefficient before using Eq.~\eqref{eq:emissivity}.
\end{implnote}

\subsection{Radiated power coefficients}

Each PEC carries the line power $\Delta E_{u\ell}\,\mathrm{PEC}$, in
W\,cm$^3$ with the photon energy $\Delta E_{u\ell}=hc/\lambda_{\rm vac}$ in J
($\times10^{-6}$ for W\,m$^3$). Summed over the lines they give, for each
driver, the low-level line power coefficient and the recombination power
coefficient (Summers et al., their Eqs.~24--25):
\begin{align}
 \mathrm{PLT}_\rho&=\sum_{u\ell}\Delta E_{u\ell}\,\mathrm{PEC}^{\rm exc}_{u\ell,\rho}
   =\sum_{u\in e}\sum_{\ell<u}\frac{\Delta E_{u\ell}A_{u\ell}F_{u\rho}}{\nelec}
    +\sum_{\ell<\rho}\frac{\Delta E_{\rho\ell}A_{\rho\ell}}{\nelec},\label{eq:plt}\\
 \mathrm{PRB}_p&=\sum_{u\ell}\Delta E_{u\ell}\,\mathrm{PEC}^{\rm rec}_{u\ell,p}
   =\sum_{u\in e}\sum_{\ell<u}\frac{\Delta E_{u\ell}A_{u\ell}G_{up}}{\nelec},\label{eq:prb}
\end{align}
with $u\in e$ the eliminated levels, and the charge-exchange power PRC$_p$ in
the same way from PEC$^{\rm CX}$. The last term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:plt} is the
radiation of the metastable $\rho$ itself, Eq.~\eqref{eq:pec-meta}. Summers
et al.\ write it as a separate term, ADAS includes it in PLT, and it dominates
for a metastable with a fast decay: for the Ne$^0$ 2p$^5$3s\,$^3$P term
($A=1.6\times10^7$\,s$^{-1}$) ColRadPy without it gave 0.02 of the ADAS PLT
(\code{plt96r}) at $10^{12}$\,cm$^{-3}$, and with it 1.00. Summers et al.\
include bremsstrahlung in PRB, and the ADAS PRB adds the recombination
continuum and the radiation among the high $n$-shells (Section~\ref{sec:adf11adas});
the \code{prb} of ColRadPy is Eq.~\eqref{eq:prb} only, the line power of the
levels of the ADF04 file after capture.

\begin{implnote}[Power outputs]
\code{processed['pls']} holds $\Delta E_{u\ell}\,\mathrm{PEC}$ for every
line and driver, and \code{plt}, \code{prb} and \code{prc} its sums over the
excitation, recombination and CX columns, in W\,cm$^3$, with the photon
energy from the level-energy difference. Every pair of levels is a line, by
lower and then upper level as in \code{pec_levels}, the lines of the retained
metastables included. The time-dependent \code{['td']['pecs']}, \code{pls}
and \code{plt} use the same lines with the time-dependent populations of all
levels, metastables included.
\end{implnote}

\subsection{S/XB: ionizations per emitted photon}\label{sec:sxb}

The diagnostic ratio S/XB relates ionization to excitation-driven line
emission~\cite{johnson}. For a single
retained driver $\rho$, define the total effective ionization coefficient
$\mathcal S_\rho=\sum_p\mathcal S_{\rho p}$. For line $u\to\ell$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sxb}
 (S/XB)_{u\ell,\rho}
    =\frac{\mathcal S_\rho}{\mathrm{PEC}^{\rm exc}_{u\ell,\rho}}.
\end{equation}
This is Eq.~23 of Summers et al.~\cite{summers2006}, the SCD of the driver
summed over the parents over its excitation PEC. Both coefficients use the
same electron-density and driving-population convention, so the ratio is
dimensionless: ionizations per excitation photon.
The numerator can instead be restricted to a specified destination parent,
provided that restricted meaning is stated.

For multiple retained populations, form the rate ratio before interpreting
it as a diagnostic:
\begin{equation}
 (S/XB)_{u\ell}[N_m]
 =\frac{\sum_\rho N_\rho\mathcal S_\rho}
        {\sum_\rho N_\rho\mathrm{PEC}^{\rm exc}_{u\ell,\rho}}.
\end{equation}
This is not an unweighted average of the individual metastable ratios.
Different metastable fractions can produce different ionization-to-photon
ratios at the same temperature and density.

If excitation dominates the observed line and S/XB is approximately constant
through the emitting column, its photon radiance $I_{u\ell}$ gives
\begin{equation}
 \int\dot n_{\rm ion}\,\dd s=(S/XB)_{u\ell}\,4\pi I_{u\ell}.
\end{equation}
If the ratio varies spatially, the local ionization rate must instead be
integrated with the corresponding emissivity. Interpreting that column
ionization rate as a surface erosion or influx rate additionally requires a
geometry and transport model: escape without ionization, repeated
recombination and ionization, and recombination/CX photons can invalidate a
simple one-ionization-per-entering-particle interpretation. A zero excitation
PEC does not define a finite ratio.

\begin{implnote}[S/XB output]
S/XB is a ratio of stored results and is not stored itself:
\routine{get_sxb} returns Eq.~\eqref{eq:sxb} for every line and retained
driver, from the parent sum of \code{scd} and the matching excitation column
of \code{pecs}, with the same normalization, and NaN where a line has no
excitation emission. \routine{write_sxb_adf13} writes it to an ADF13
file~\cite{adf13}, as ADAS208 does, an undefined ratio as the format's floor
$10^{-74}$ with a warning; the file needs a temperature--density grid. Both
need ionization in the calculation. A per-driver photon rate in
s$^{-1}$ must not be mixed with an ionization coefficient in cm$^3$/s.
\end{implnote}

\section{Wavelengths, fine structure and line profiles}\label{sec:spectra}

\subsection{Wavelengths and NIST energy matching}

Vacuum wavelength follows Eq.~\eqref{eq:conversion}. For a chosen air
convention, $\lambda_{\rm air}=\lambda_{\rm vac}/n(\lambda_{\rm vac})$.
The air-wavelength helper uses a pressure- and temperature-adjusted
dispersion expression:
\begin{align}
 D(\xi)&=8342.54+\frac{2406147}{130-\xi^2}
                       +\frac{15998}{38.9-\xi^2},\quad
 \xi=1000/\lambda_{\rm vac,nm},\\
 n&=1+10^{-8}\!\left[D(\xi)\frac{P}{96095.43}
 \frac{1+10^{-8}(0.601-0.00972T_C)P}{1+0.0036610T_C}
 -P_w(0.037345-0.000401\xi^2)\right].
\end{align}
Here $P$ and water-vapor pressure $P_w$ are in Pa and $T_C$ is in Celsius;
the defaults are 101325 Pa, 0 Pa and 15\,$^\circ$C.
This empirical expression is not a wavelength-independent physical law.
It has mathematical poles near 87.706 and 160.334 nm. ColRadPy keeps
vacuum wavelengths below 200 nm and applies an air conversion only above
that threshold. An optical air convention is thus separate from the vacuum
convention used for UV emission.

NIST matching changes line positions by replacing matched level energies.
It does not recompute collision strengths or establish that LS splitting
reproduces a resolved collisional radiative calculation. Configurations,
term labels, $J$ values, and ambiguous repeated labels must be matched
consistently. The split structure is constructed when needed before the
levels are shifted.

\subsection{Statistical splitting of LS multiplets}

For a spin-allowed electric-dipole LS transition, relative component
strengths can be expressed through an angular factor
\begin{equation}
 W_{J_uJ_\ell}\ \propto\ (2J_u+1)(2J_\ell+1)
 \begin{Bmatrix}L_u&J_u&S\\J_\ell&L_\ell&1\end{Bmatrix}^{\!2}.
\end{equation}
Normalize the allowed factors over the multiplet and distribute the term
PEC by $\mathrm{PEC}_{J_uJ_\ell}=\mathrm{PEC}_{\rm LS}W/\sum W$.
This describes relative LS angular strengths with the associated statistical
population assumption. It does not add resolved-$J$ collisional dynamics or
configuration mixing. \routine{split_pec_multiplet} evaluates equivalent
Condon--Shortley factors and renormalizes them for spin-conserving
dipole multiplets ($|\Delta L|\le1$). Intercombination lines and lines with
$|\Delta L|>1$, which these factors do not describe, are kept unsplit at the
wavelength between the statistically weighted term centres.

\subsection{Doppler and natural profiles}

For an emitter of mass $m$ at temperature $T_i$,
\begin{equation}
 \Delta\nu_D=\frac{\nu_0}{c}\sqrt{\frac{2eT_i}{m}},\qquad
 \phi_D(\nu)=\frac{\exp[-(\nu-\nu_0)^2/\Delta\nu_D^2]}
                   {\sqrt\pi\Delta\nu_D}.
\end{equation}
This is a Gaussian with standard deviation $\Delta\nu_D/\sqrt2$ and FWHM
$2\sqrt{\ln2}\,\Delta\nu_D$. The broadening API takes $T_i$ in eV and
the emitter mass in kg.

For lifetime broadening of an upper and lower state,
\begin{equation}
 \Delta\nu_L=\frac{\Gamma_u+\Gamma_\ell}{2\pi},\qquad
 \gamma=\frac{\Delta\nu_L}{2},\qquad
 \phi_L(\nu)=\frac{\gamma/\pi}{(\nu-\nu_0)^2+\gamma^2}.
\end{equation}
The Lorentzian FWHM is $\Delta\nu_L$. Density-dependent pressure broadening
requires additional physics; it is not supplied merely by this lifetime width.

For a profile tabulated per wavelength rather than per frequency, preserve
area with $\phi_\lambda=\phi_\nu|\dd\nu/\dd\lambda|$ and consistent length
units. The code evaluates a frequency-shaped kernel on a wavelength grid and
renormalizes its finite-grid area numerically. That construction is a useful
narrow-line profile but should not be assumed identical to an exact
Jacobian-transformed wide profile. Grid width and resolution control omitted
wings and numerical normalization.

\begin{implnote}[Profile grid resolution]
Natural profiles are evaluated on offsets $\Delta\lambda$ from the line
centre, stored in \code{dwave_arr}, with the frequency offsets
$\Delta\nu=-c\,\Delta\lambda/[\lambda_0(\lambda_0+\Delta\lambda)]$. A natural
width can be far below the float64 resolution of the wavelength itself
($5\times10^{-15}$\,nm for the Be\,I line at 454.8\,nm, where float64 resolves
$6\times10^{-14}$\,nm), and frequencies near $10^{15}$\,Hz are resolved only to
0.1\,Hz, so neither absolute grid can carry such profiles. The absolute
wavelengths \code{wave_arr} of these lines coincide.
\end{implnote}

\section{Time-dependent evolution and matrix exponentials}\label{sec:time}

For fixed plasma conditions and an optional constant source $s$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ode}
 \dot N=CN+s,\qquad
 N(t)=\ee^{Ct}N(0)+\int_0^t\ee^{C(t-u)}s\,\dd u.
\end{equation}
If $C=V\Lambda V^{-1}$ has a complete eigenbasis, the source-free result is
$V\ee^{\Lambda t}V^{-1}N(0)$. Each source-driven mode obeys
\begin{equation}
 v_k(t)=v_k(0)\ee^{\lambda_kt}+c_k\phi(\lambda_k,t),\quad
 c=V^{-1}s,\quad
 \phi(\lambda,t)=\begin{cases}
  (\ee^{\lambda t}-1)/\lambda,&\lambda\ne0,\\t,&\lambda=0.
 \end{cases}\label{eq:source-mode}
\end{equation}
For small $|\lambda t|$, evaluating the numerator with \code{expm1} avoids
subtracting nearly equal floating-point numbers. Small $|\lambda|$ by itself
does not make a mode negligible: its decay is governed by $|\lambda t|$.
An alternative formulation is the augmented exponential
\begin{equation}\label{eq:augmented}
 \begin{pmatrix}N(t)\\1\end{pmatrix}
 =\exp\!\left[t\begin{pmatrix}C&s\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\right]
                 \begin{pmatrix}N(0)\\1\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
For constant $C$, these formulas propagate the initial state at $t=0$;
the first requested output time is not a new integration origin. The initial
vector is a physical assumption about the species: atoms leaving a surface
start in the ground state, and their emission evolves as metastable populations
build up~\cite{johnson}. The
rate equations accept any initial vector, and the time to form metastables
corresponds to a distance from the surface only with a model of the particle
motion and geometry.

\subsection{Complex modes and real populations}

A real, nonsymmetric rate matrix can have complex conjugate eigenvalues.
For example, a conservative three-state cycle has
\begin{equation}
 C=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0&1\\1&-1&0\\0&1&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 \lambda=0,\ -\tfrac32\pm\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\,\mathrm i.
\end{equation}
The complex modes are legitimate and must be retained in the reconstruction.
For real initial data and a real source, their imaginary contributions cancel
in the exact populations. Taking the real part of eigenvectors or eigenvalues
changes the dynamics. Taking the real part of a reconstructed population is
justified only after a numerical assessment of its imaginary residual.
Real-arithmetic matrix-exponential methods avoid those complex intermediates.

\subsection{Defective and ill-conditioned eigenbases}

Not every valid generator is diagonalizable. An equal-rate chain is a useful
counterexample:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chain}
 C=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0&0\\1&-1&0\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
 N(0)=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix},\quad
 N(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\ee^{-t}\\t\ee^{-t}\\1-(1+t)\ee^{-t}\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The $t\ee^{-t}$ term comes from a Jordan block. A nominal inverse of numerical
eigenvectors can return plausible values that omit this term. Near a defective
matrix, eigenvector conditioning matters more than the visual separation of
eigenvalues alone. A robust algorithm should assess that conditioning and use
a matrix-exponential or equivalent stable method when needed.

\begin{implnote}[Transient algorithms]
The transient solvers check eigenvector conditioning and compare selected
populations against a matrix exponential. Untrusted points are recomputed
with \routine{scipy.linalg.expm}; a constant source uses an augmented matrix.
Real populations are returned, and slow physical modes are retained according
to their evolution over the requested times rather than an absolute rate
cutoff. The cached evaluator needs the original rate matrix to perform the
same exponential fallback.
\end{implnote}

\subsection{Stationary solutions}

A normalized stationary population of a closed model satisfies
\begin{equation}
 C\pi=0,\qquad \vb1^{\mathsf T}\pi=1,\qquad \pi_i\ge0.
\end{equation}
For a unique equilibrium, one redundant balance equation can be replaced by
the normalization equation. Rescaling balance equations helps avoid loss of
weak-rate constraints. If multiple disconnected closed classes exist, their
relative weights depend on the initial condition; normalization alone does
not determine a unique distribution.

A rate matrix that conserves particles (non-negative off-diagonal rates and
columns that sum to zero) is solved by Grassmann--Taksar--Heyman elimination,
which uses only the off-diagonal rates and has no subtractions, so every
population keeps the relative accuracy of the rates however small it is.
Solutions through the eigenvectors or a linear solve are accurate only relative
to the largest population: for a resolved carbon balance at 10\,eV they gave
C$^{5+}$ $2\times10^{-5}$ where the exact value is $3\times10^{-16}$. For other
matrices the stationary solver tries the smallest-magnitude eigenmode and
checks its residual and normalization; an unreliable projection is replaced by
a normalized stationary solve. The ionization-balance wrapper calls this
routine directly.

\section{Ionization balance across charge stages}\label{sec:ionbalance}

Collect all retained metastables across the modeled stages into a vector $x$.
At fixed plasma parameters,
\begin{equation}
 \dot x=Mx+s,\qquad M=\nelec B.
\end{equation}
Here $B$ is a coefficient matrix with units cm$^3$/s. Its adjacent-stage
blocks contain SCD upward and ACD downward. QCD connects retained levels
within the lower stage, and XCD contributes connections within the parent
stage through the lower stage. A given stage can therefore receive both its
own QCD and XCD contributions computed from the preceding charge state.

For each off-diagonal transition, the same coefficient is subtracted from
the source diagonal. Consequently $\vb1^{\mathsf T}B=0$ in a closed
calculation. The CX downward blocks use
$(\nh/\nelec)\mathcal C$ inside $B$, so the final multiplication by electron
density recovers the physical multiplier $\nh$.

\begin{implnote}
\routine{populate_ion_matrix} stores $B$ in \code{ion_matrix}, despite that
name suggesting a density-weighted rate. \routine{solve_no_source} and
\routine{solve_source} multiply by the electron-density grid before invoking
the matrix-exponential solvers. Do not apply the density multiplier a second
time when interpreting their populations.
\end{implnote}

\subsection{Ionization age}

For constant electron density, define $\tau=\nelec t$. If
$B$ has no density dependence, a solution at fixed temperature can be written
as $x(\tau)=\exp(B\tau)x(0)$. In a full GCR model, quenching and three-body
terms can make $B$ density-dependent, so equal ionization age does not
necessarily imply equal charge-state fractions at different densities.

\begin{implnote}[Ionization-age grids]
The wrapper constructs times as $t=\tau/\nelec$ with axes
\emph{density, time} when there are multiple densities; one density uses a
one-dimensional time array. This agrees with the matrix-exponential solver's
grid convention, including singleton axes.
\end{implnote}

\subsection{Input routes and retained manifolds}\label{sec:inputroutes}

\routine{ionization_balance} can build coefficients from ADF04 models or read
precomputed ADF11/HDF5 data. The number of retained metastables is a
stage-dependent quantity. A ragged collection such as $[\,[0,1],\,[0,1,2],]$
must not be forced into a rectangular numeric array. The order of input files
also carries charge-stage information and should be explicit in a calculation
record. The current readers and constructors impose additional restrictions
listed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\begin{implnote}[Constructor defaults]
For ADF04, a flat metastable array selects levels in the first stage; later
selections use the preceding SCD parent count. A per-stage list may have
unequal lengths. Default initial abundances are sized after the GCR stages
are loaded, including the final parent stage, and place all population in
the first state. ADF11 and HDF5 source solves use the same default-abundance
helper after their ionization matrix is constructed.
\end{implnote}

\subsection{Stage-to-stage coefficients}\label{sec:stage}

A stage-to-stage balance follows one population per charge stage, with the
coefficients of conventional collisional-radiative models: SCD, ACD, CCD, PLT,
PRB and PRC for each stage, and no QCD or XCD, which move population between
the metastables of one stage or between parents~\cite{summers2006}. They can
be made from the GCR picture in two ways.

Retaining only the ground condenses the metastables onto the ground, which
restores the ground-state collisional-radiative picture of Bates et
al.~\cite{summers2006}. Each metastable becomes an excited level whose
excitation-driven population enters the stage normalization
(Section~\ref{sec:normalization}) and the SCD, while its capture-driven
population is not counted in the stage, and the ACD counts only captures that
reach the ground.

Alternatively, the resolved coefficients are contracted with the metastable
fractions $f_\rho=m_\rho/\sum_\sigma m_\sigma$ of the ionizing stage, and
$f^+_p$ of the recombining stage, as ADAS404 makes its unresolved ADF11 files
(Section~\ref{sec:adf11adas}):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:unresolved}
 \mathcal S_{\rm stage}=\sum_\rho f_\rho\sum_p\mathcal S_{\rho p},\qquad
 \mathrm{PLT}_{\rm stage}=\sum_\rho f_\rho\,\mathrm{PLT}_\rho,\qquad
 \mathcal A_{\rm stage}=\sum_p f^+_p\sum_\rho\mathcal A_{\rho p}.
\end{equation}
The fractions tell how the population of a stage is shared among its
metastables, which a single population per stage cannot track. The ratios
$m_\rho$ to the ground ($m_g=1$), the MET data of ADAS, are the steady state
of the retained metastables of the stage alone: excitation, de-excitation and
their ionization loss, without capture from the next stage. For a ground $g$
and one metastable $\mu$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:met}
 m_\mu=\frac{\mathcal Q_{g\to\mu}}
            {\mathcal Q_{\mu\to g}+\sum_p\mathcal S_{\mu p}},
\end{equation}
and a linear system for more metastables. In a plasma the metastable is also
fed by capture, and in steady state
\begin{equation}\label{eq:met-plasma}
 \frac{N_\mu}{N_g}=m_\mu
   +\frac{\sum_p\mathcal A_{\mu p}N^+_p}
         {(\mathcal Q_{\mu\to g}+\sum_p\mathcal S_{\mu p})\,N_g}.
\end{equation}
The contraction neglects the second term, which holds in an ionizing plasma,
where the next stage is scarce. Where the next stage dominates, most
metastable atoms come from capture and are ionized again before they reach
the ground. Equation~\eqref{eq:unresolved} then weights the ionization with
too small a metastable share; metastables ionize more readily than the
ground, so the stage-to-stage SCD is too small and the population of the
stage too large. The condensation onto the ground errs the other way: it
leaves the capture-fed metastable atoms out of the stage, whose population
from ACD/SCD is then too small. Retaining the metastables avoids both
assumptions. Summers et al.\ also note that XCD is needed to build a
meaningful stage-to-stage ionization coefficient from the GCR
ones~\cite{summers2006}: it moves population between the metastables of the
next stage through this one, and the contraction leaves it out of the
fractions of that stage, where it would add to its QCD.

\routine{ionization_balance} makes the contraction from ADF04 inputs with
\code{use_met=True} (default \code{False}): each stage is solved with its
metastables, \routine{metastable_fractions} gives their fractions from its QCD
and SCD, Eq.~\eqref{eq:unresolved} gives one SCD, ACD, CCD and PLT per stage,
and the balance is solved stage to stage. It reproduces ADAS's unresolved data
where ADAS's own data are consistent, but it is not more accurate than the
condensation onto the ground. MET only counts metastable atoms made by excitation from
the ground. In He$^0$, most recombinations land on the triplets, and the
1s2s\,$^3$S metastable is easily ionized, so most of those atoms go straight
back to He$^+$. The collapse counts them as recombined but never as
re-ionized, so He$^0$ comes out too abundant. Be$^0$'s $^3$P holds more atoms
than the ground, so there the condensation's error dominates instead:
it drops the recombination-fed $^3$P atoms from the stage entirely. Against
the equilibrium $N(\mathrm X^0)/N(\mathrm X^+)$ of the resolved balance
(1--100\,eV, $10^{10}$--$10^{14}$\,cm$^{-3}$, ADAS's ADF04, ADF17 and ADF18
files: \texttt{cpb03\_ls\#be0.dat} and \texttt{mom97\_ls\#he0.dat} with their
\texttt{cbnm96} and \texttt{exp96} projection), the collapse gives
1.00--1.34 for Be$^0$ and 1.02--2.21 for He$^0$, the condensation onto the
ground 0.26--1.00 and 0.88--1.00. So no stage-to-stage model is right for every ion.
The physically correct option is the resolved balance, the default.

\subsection{Electron energy loss, radiated power and emission functions}\label{sec:eloss}

A fluid energy equation needs the energy that the free electrons lose to an
ion. Summers et al.\ (their Eq.~27) write it from the GCR coefficients and the
power coefficients,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:energy-loss}
 L_e=\sum_XE_X\,\dot N_X\big|_{\rm atomic}
     +\nelec\sum_\rho\mathrm{PLT}_\rho N_\rho+\nelec\sum_p\mathrm{PRB}_pN_p,
\end{equation}
where $X$ runs over the metastables of the ion and of its parent, $E_X$ is the
absolute energy of $X$, and $\dot N_X|_{\rm atomic}$ its rate of change by the
GCR coefficients: transfer between the metastables, ionization and
recombination. With PLT as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:plt} it includes the forbidden
lines between metastables. Only relative energies remain, because the atomic
processes conserve particles, and in ionization equilibrium the first term
vanishes: the electron energy loss equals the radiated power. For an element in
ionization equilibrium the radiated power coefficient is (their Eq.~53)
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ptot}
 P^{\rm tot}=\sum_z\sum_\sigma\bigl(\mathrm{PLT}^{[z]}_\sigma+\mathrm{PRB}^{[z]}_\sigma\bigr)
   \Bigl(\frac{N^{[z]}_\sigma}{N^{\rm tot}}\Bigr)_{\rm eq},
\end{equation}
in W\,cm$^3$ per electron and per ion of the element, where
PRB$^{[z]}_\sigma$ is the recombination power driven by the metastable
$\sigma$ of stage $z$, which stage $z-1$ emits. The emission functions of a
line, the GTN functions of Summers et al.\ (their Eq.~54, the $G(T_e)$ of solar
physics at finite density), combine the PECs with the same fractions:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gtn}
 \mathrm{GTN}^{[z]}_{u\ell}=\sum_\sigma\mathrm{PEC}^{[z]\,\rm exc}_{u\ell,\sigma}
   \Bigl(\frac{N^{[z]}_\sigma}{N^{\rm tot}}\Bigr)_{\rm eq}
  +\sum_p\mathrm{PEC}^{[z]\,\rm rec}_{u\ell,p}
   \Bigl(\frac{N^{[z+1]}_p}{N^{\rm tot}}\Bigr)_{\rm eq}.
\end{equation}
ColRadPy provides the coefficients and, from \routine{ionization_balance}, the
equilibrium metastable fractions (\code{processed['pops_ss']}), but does not
assemble Eqs.~\eqref{eq:energy-loss}--\eqref{eq:gtn}. Its \code{prb} lacks
the continuum and bremsstrahlung that the ADAS PRB supplies.

\section{Charge-state energy balance}\label{sec:energy}

The energy-balance extension first solves the charge-state fractions $x_z(t)$,
then evolves a kinetic-energy variable per modeled particle,
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal E_z=\frac32x_zT_z.
\end{equation}
It transports that variable between charge states with the same ionization
matrix and adds exchange with a prescribed background ion bath at $T_b$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:energy}
 \dot{\mathcal E}_z=\sum_{z'}M_{zz'}\mathcal E_{z'}
      -\frac23\nu_z\mathcal E_z+\nu_zx_zT_b.
\end{equation}
The convention for $\nu_z$ is an energy-transfer frequency:
$\tfrac32x_z\dot T_z=-\nu_zx_z(T_z-T_b)$ when the population is fixed.
The temperature-relaxation frequency is therefore $2\nu_z/3$.
This is kinetic-temperature exchange, not a complete plasma energy equation
including radiation, electron heating, and ionization-potential energy losses
(Section~\ref{sec:eloss}).

\subsection{Charged and neutral energy transfer}

The charged helper implements
\begin{align}
 v_s^2&=2eT_s/m_s,\\
 \nu_{ab}&=\frac{n_b Z_a^2Z_b^2e^4\ln\Lambda}
 {\pi^{3/2}\epsilon_0^2m_am_b(v_a^2+v_b^2)^{3/2}}.
\end{align}
In these expressions temperatures are numeric values in eV, $eT$ is an SI
energy, masses are in kg, and $n_b$ is in m$^{-3}$. The helper evaluates
\begin{align}
 \lambda_D^{-2}&=\sum_s\frac{n_sZ_s^2e^2}{\epsilon_0 eT_s},\\
 b_{\rm cl}&=\frac{|Z_aZ_b|e^2}
 {4\pi\epsilon_0\mu(v_a^2+v_b^2)/2}, &
 b_{\rm q}&=\frac{\hbar}{\mu\sqrt{v_a^2+v_b^2}},\\
 \ln\Lambda&=\ln\frac{\lambda_D}{\max(b_{\rm cl},b_{\rm q})},
 \qquad \mu=\frac{m_am_b}{m_a+m_b}.
\end{align}
The screened, weakly coupled collision model is not reliable when its
assumptions fail or its logarithm ceases to be physically meaningful.

For the neutral, the implemented polarization-scattering approximation uses
\begin{equation}
 K_{\rm mt}=2.21\pi\alpha_{\rm f}c
     \sqrt{\frac{m_e}{\mu}\,a_{\rm pol}a_0^4},\qquad
 \nu_{nb}=\frac{3n_b\mu}{m_n+m_b}K_{\rm mt}.
\end{equation}
Here $a_{\rm pol}$ is the numeric polarizability in Bohr-radius cubed and
$\alpha_{\rm f}$ is the fine-structure constant. This elastic approximation
does not include every long-range or inelastic energy-transfer mechanism.

\begin{caveat}[Energy extension]
The caller converts electron density from cm$^{-3}$ to m$^{-3}$ for the
Debye helper. The energy solver uses one temperature/density point,
reconstructs populations from stored eigenpairs, and integrates
Eq.~\eqref{eq:energy} with BDF.
A failed integration gives a warning with the solver message, and the
stored times, abundances, energies and temperatures stop at the last time
reached.
\end{caveat}

\section{Data products and external interfaces}\label{sec:interfaces}

\subsection{Array layout}\label{sec:arrays}

For an ordinary temperature--density grid, thermodynamic axes trail the
physical indices: $(\ldots,N_T,N_n)$. Paired mode uses
$(\ldots,N_{\rm pair})$, evaluating corresponding temperature and density
entries rather than their Cartesian product. Typical arrays are
\begin{align*}
 \code{pops}&:\ (N_e,N_{\rm driver},N_T,N_n),\\
 \code{pecs}&:\ (N_{\rm line},N_{\rm driver},N_T,N_n),\\
 \code{scd},\code{acd}&:\ (N_m,N_p,N_T,N_n),\\
 \code{qcd}&:\ (N_m,N_m,N_T,N_n),\\
 \code{xcd}&:\ (N_p,N_p,N_T,N_n).
\end{align*}
The paired layout removes one grid axis. An excited-level axis is not a
physical bound-level index; use the stored level map. Likewise the driving
column index combines several process types and does not universally mean
an ionization parent. The index order of the effective coefficients is:

\subsection{ADF04, ADF11, ADF13, ADF15 and HDF5}

ADF04 is a level-resolved atomic-data format, whereas ADF11 holds effective
charge-state coefficients on temperature and density grids, made as described
in Section~\ref{sec:adf11adas}. The ADF11 reader
stores logarithmic data and the ionization-balance path interpolates the
logged grids and coefficients before converting to physical values.
File naming and block interpretation identify coefficient families.
ADF13 holds S/XB ratios of selected lines in blocks like those of ADF15,
each with its driver~\cite{adf13}; ColRadPy reads them and writes its own
from the SCD and excitation PECs (Section~\ref{sec:sxb}).

ADF15 records line photon emission coefficients with wavelength and driver
identifiers. Its wavelength convention is Angstroms~\cite{adf15}, so a
conversion from internal nm must occur exactly once. The number of output
blocks must include every emitted line--driver pair; each block needs an
unambiguous index and process/driver metadata. These are part of the scientific
interface because a downstream reader can silently drop or misidentify lines.

\begin{implnote}[Export and reading]
The ADF15 wrapper converts nm to Angstroms once; the writer declares every
line--driver block and assigns distinct selectors, and writes the ion field
(\texttt{C + 2}) and a source member name, without which the ADAS reader stops.
The ADF13 writer uses the same fields, with \texttt{INDM = T} for populations
normalized to the whole ion and the driver index otherwise; the ADAS reader
keeps three digits of the selector, so at most 999 blocks are read.
The ADF11 constructor collects coefficient files before filling missing
QCD/XCD arrays, so their order does not require SCD first. The readers follow
the fixed fields of the ADAS specifications: ADF04 values are 8-character
fields, so a negative value can follow the previous one without a space;
ADF11 blocks are placed by their labels (IPRT, IGRD, JPRT, JGRD), whose order
and meaning change with the class, including metastable resolved power files;
each ADF13 block has its own grids and metastable. They were checked against
the ADAS data collections: every ADF11 and ADF13 file, the condensed
projection ADF17 files and a sample of ADF04 files from every collection.
\end{implnote}

The HDF5 dumping method uses an optional serialization dependency and can
select PECs, GCR coefficients, and additional arrays. Record the data version,
source file and hash, selected metastables, units, grid ordering, normalization
convention, and any rate supplementation with each saved result.

\subsection{How ADAS makes its ADF11 data}\label{sec:adf11adas}

ADF11 is a derived format~\cite{adf11}: its coefficients, functions of the
electron temperature and density, come from collisional-radiative models and
are given by element at two levels of refinement~\cite{summers2006}. In the
\emph{unresolved} or stage-to-stage data only the ground states of the ions
are taken as dominant populations: these are conventional
collisional-radiative (CR) coefficients, one per charge stage, of the classes
ACD, SCD, CCD, PLT, PRB and PRC (for example \code{scd96}, \code{acd96}). In
the \emph{metastable resolved} data the ground and the metastables of each ion
may be dominant: these are the generalized collisional-radiative (GCR)
coefficients of Section~\ref{sec:gcr}, the same classes with the block of each ion
subdivided by metastable (and parent), plus QCD and XCD (for example
\code{scd96r}, \code{qcd96r}, \code{xcd96r}). QCD and XCD exist only for GCR
data, because they move population between the metastables of one stage or
between parents, which a single population per stage does not have. Three codes
make the GCR-project data (classes \code{96}). ADAS204~\cite{adas204} solves a bundle-$nS$ model and writes the
condensed matrices that Section~\ref{sec:projection} projects onto the low
levels. ADAS208~\cite{adas208} solves the model of an ADF04 file with that
projection and partitions the reduced matrix into the coefficients above:
the ionization of each level is that of the S lines of the file, or ECIP
without them, optionally replaced by zero-density ADF07 rates for the
metastables, and three-body recombination follows from it by detailed
balance, as in ColRadPy. The results are stored per isoelectronic sequence
(ADF10~\cite{adf10}) on the scaled grids $\Te/z_1^2$ and $\nelec/z_1^7$, $z_1$
being the charge of the recombining ion, and ADAS404~\cite{adas404} makes the
ADF11 files by cubic-spline interpolation in $z_1$, temperature and density.
PRB does not come from ADAS208: it is the bundle-$n$ recombination power of
ADAS204, the recombination continuum, bremsstrahlung weighted by spin system,
the cascade and dielectronic stabilization, whereas \code{prb} in ColRadPy is
the line power of the levels of the ADF04 file after capture only.

The unresolved coefficients of the GCR project (classes \code{96}, and
\code{93} from the earlier resolved data), although described as conventional
CR data, are not made with a separate model: ADAS404 contracts the resolved
ones with metastable fractions, Eq.~\eqref{eq:unresolved}. The contraction
changes only the stages with more than one metastable: in the \code{96r} files
these are, for example, He$^0$, Li$^+$, Be$^0$ and Be$^{2+}$, and all but the
H-, Li- and Na-like stages of C, N, O, Ne and Si. The fractions are an ADF10
class of their own (MET, ``equilibrium metastable fractions'',
\texttt{adf10/met96/pj\#met96\_<seq>\#\#.dat}, written by ADAS208 for each
ion), which holds the ratios $m_\rho=N_\rho/N_g$ to the ground (for Be$^0$
$m_{^3\mathrm P}=1.49$ at 2\,eV) and which ADAS404 normalizes to fractions
after interpolation. Other unresolved classes (\code{74}, \code{85},
\code{89}, \code{92}, \code{12}, \code{50}) come from other codes without
resolved data; the code that made a file is named in its comments.

For line emission ``unresolved'' means the condensation onto the ground
instead: ADAS's unresolved photon emissivity and S/XB files (prefixes
\code{pju}, \code{llu}; \code{INDM = T}) come from an ADAS208 run with the
ground as the only metastable and the populations normalized to the whole
stage, its resolved ones (\code{pjr}, \code{llr}) from a run with the
metastables. ColRadPy reproduces the former with the ground as its only
metastable.

\section{Analytic examples and checks}\label{sec:checks}

\subsection{A two-level density scan}

For a two-level ion with upward and downward coefficients $q_{01},q_{10}$
and radiative rate $A$, the stationary, ion-total-normalized excited fraction is
\begin{equation}
 f_1=\frac{\nelec q_{01}}{A+\nelec(q_{01}+q_{10})},\qquad
 \mathrm{PEC}_{10}=\frac{Aq_{01}}{A+\nelec(q_{01}+q_{10})}.
\end{equation}
At low density, PEC approaches $q_{01}$ and $f_1$ is proportional to density.
At high density, $f_1\to q_{01}/(q_{01}+q_{10})$ while PEC falls inversely
with density. The photon rate per emitting ion approaches $Af_1$, not zero.
This distinction explains why a falling PEC is not itself vanishing emission.

solution. An independent SciPy matrix exponential reproduces it; the
ColRadPy solver agrees with both it and the analytic
solution while conserving population.

\section{Validity boundaries and implementation checklist}\label{sec:limitations}

\subsection{Physical assumptions}

The model assumes a specified Maxwellian electron bath, rates appropriate
to the supplied atomic resolution, and escaping photons. The quasistatic
approximation additionally requires fast excited-state relaxation.
Statistical LS multiplet splitting assumes the relevant coupling and
population pattern. ECIP approximates missing ionization; extrapolation
does not create new atomic accuracy. The energy extension describes trace
species kinetics in a prescribed background. These assumptions are separate
from implementation defects.

The table is a guide to interpreting the current implementation. Before using a result,
check coefficient signs, state and parent maps, column-sum conservation,
population normalization, line-energy consistency, density powers, and the
exported units. For a transient solve, compare against an independent matrix
exponential at representative grid points and verify the initial condition.
For a quasistatic solve, substitute the calculated excited populations back
into the balance equation. Agreement with these identities does not establish
the accuracy of the input atomic calculations, but a failure identifies a
problem before comparison with an experiment.
